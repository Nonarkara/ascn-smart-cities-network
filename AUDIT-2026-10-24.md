# Audit: The Communication Round, 24 October 2026

> Historical handoff record. The completed 28 October release audit and current
> dispositions are in [AUDIT-2026-10-28.md](AUDIT-2026-10-28.md). In particular,
> its News and Innovation geometry repairs supersede the remaining findings below.

This review examines reading order, onward navigation and content-sized geometry
in the observatory. Its evidence comes from the rendered interface and the
measurements recorded below.

Two things were wrong with the site against that brief, and both were
measured before anything was built.

**The site communicated a catalogue, not a path.** Review three counted the
onward affordances on every view: ten of the fifteen views carried no link
to any other view at all. Archive, projects, history, framework, partners,
contacts, insights, library, research and essay were dead ends. Only the
overview stated a path, and only the news view offered a rich set of onward
choices. The rail's numbering was the only universal orientation, and the
reviewer was right that "next in the rail" is not "next in the argument":
the numbering runs across four groups (Intelligence 01–04, Observatory
05–08, Network 09–11, Knowledge 12–15), so 03 The Record sits in
Intelligence while 05 History opens Observatory.

**The information bled where a container grew faster than its content.** The
same review listed the offenders by ratio, and they were concentrated in
section chrome rather than diffuse: a bordered, filled callout panel 75%
empty; six "open questions" rows each ruled 1,190px past their sentence; a
pull-quote whose bracket was 3.94× the words it bracketed; a contact link
underlined 4.50× its length; a badge stretched to 6.27× its ink.

This round answers the first and closes the second.

## The onward path band

Every view now ends with a **path band**: a closed band carrying the next two
views in the reading circuit, each as `[number] [name] [one-line descriptor]`,
under a `NEXT` kicker. The band is the last element of the view it belongs
to, and it is the only thing the reader needs in order to decide where to go
next.

- **The order is the rail's own spine.** Overview → 02 Innovation → 03 The
  Record → 04 News → 05 History → 06 Cities → 07 Projects → 08 Framework →
  09 Partners → 10 Contacts → 11 Insights → 12 Library → 13 Archive →
  14 Research → 15 Essay → back to 01 Overview. The reading order is a
  circuit: the last two views wrap back to the start. The ASCN9 view, which
  the rail does not list, reads from the Overview's position.
- **The hierarchy is three steps, and the destination leads.** The name is
  19px at 390 and 24px above it (the house's `--t-lead` step, so the ladder
  does not grow), the ordinal is 11px and quiet, the descriptor is 11px. This
  is the correction an independent review forced: the band was built with the
  number at 24px, weight 800, in the only saturated colour on the band, and
  the name at 13px in plain ink. A squint test — the band scaled to 120px —
  showed that only "02" and "03" were legible, and the destination names
  dissolved. The same token ("02") is 10px and muted in the index rail; a
  number cannot be the loudest thing in one place and the quietest in the
  next.
- **The descriptors say what is in the destination, never the destination's
  own name again.** Each is a short phrase — "Service models", "Public
  reporting", "Regional dispatches", "Atlas and profiles" — translated with
  the rest of the site. The old line repeated the name inside the name
  ("Innovation" over "Innovation-as-a-Service · Digital Twins"), which reads
  as a rendering fault rather than as a hierarchy, and it carried the view's
  own headline numbers, which the view's headline and its stat pair already
  said on the same screen.
- **It is a closed band, because two signposts cannot fill a column.** The
  band is `width: fit-content; max-width: 100%` with a 2px rule above it: a
  full-width frame around 443–534px of content would be exactly the invented
  space this round is about. Its box equals its ink (reach 1.000) at every
  width measured.
- **Its right end ends with the last word.** The two signposts sat in equal
  `1fr` columns, so the band's right edge was set by the *longer* signpost's
  track and the 2px rule ran past the last word — a rule that appeared to
  run into nothing. The columns now hug their own content (`auto`, which is
  `minmax(min-content, max-content)`, so they can still shrink to the
  longest word when the view is too narrow for them). Measured over 16 views
  × 3 editions × 4 widths: the void past the last glyph is **0.00px** in
  every case.

  The earlier draft of this record attributed a **35.29px** hole in English
  (43.91 in Thai, 48.00 in Chinese) to the column rule. The sixth review's
  counterfactual shows that attribution was wrong: restoring the old
  `repeat(2, minmax(0,1fr))` **and** the default `justify-content` — the old
  rule in full, not half of it — on today's content leaves **2.34px** in
  English, 59.31px in Thai and 22.00px in Chinese (News view, 1440). The
  hole was the equal-track slack, and it is worst in Thai, where the two
  signposts are closest in length and the equal tracks waste the most.
- **The destination names are links, and they look like links.** The names
  wore `--ink` and carried no affordance at rest; the only signal that they
  went somewhere was an amber rule on hover, which a reader on a phone
  never reaches. They wear `--accent` now — the house's declared
  wayfinding-and-links colour — and the rule that divides the steps takes
  it on hover, while the name takes the underline the house gives every
  other link.

### The accent has one job now

The fifth and sixth reviews both found the same thing from opposite ends:
the News screen used **one colour for four different jobs**. Inside the
band, on hover, amber meant "go here next". On the same screen it also
meant "you are here" (the rail's active row), "this is the active filter"
(the review tab's underline and the topic button's underline) and "this is
an interpretation" (ten `.review-record-detail` left rules). A colour that
means four things is a colour that means nothing.

Amber leaves the band: it keeps the meaning the rail, the tabs and the
topic filter already agree on — *this is the item you are on*. The band and
the chrome's cue share `--accent`, and `--accent` is declared for wayfinding
and links. After this round the stylesheet uses `--accent` in exactly two
components: the chrome's route cue and the path band's destination names.

Stated exactly, amber on the News screen means **the selected state**: the
active sub-view tab, the pressed topic filter, the active rail row. It
also fills data marks (the country bars) and the hover state of a button,
which are a different register — a magnitude and an affordance, not a
selection. The seventh review noted that an earlier draft of this record
claimed amber meant "exactly one thing", and that was an overstatement: it
is one *state*, and the data and hover uses are not states.

**That claim did not survive the sixth review, and the review was right.**
Ten `.review-record-detail` left rules on the News screen were 3px of amber,
and they meant *this is an interpretation* — a fourth meaning for the same
colour, on the same screen, in the same component the record had just
declared monochrome. The bracket is now the house's strong rule instead:
2px of `--ink`, the weight the method block above it already uses. The
border lost a pixel and the padding took it back, so the interpretation's
text does not move: 16.00px from the card's edge before, 16.00px after. A
1px shift would have been a failed attempt under Law VII, not an emphasis.

## The route line

The band states the next two destinations at the **end** of a view. An
independent review measured how far away that is: the band sits 3.7 to 25
viewport-heights below the top of the plate, so it answers "where next" only
for a reader who has already finished the view. The brief asked for the next
step to be understood "within the shortest amount of time", and the only
part of the chrome that is on screen in every view is the strip under the
plate bar.

So that strip is now the **route line**:

```
01  OVERVIEW   ›  02  INNOVATION
```

- **Left: where you are.** The view's number and its name, from the same
  dictionary entries the rail uses, so the strip and the index can never
  disagree. It used to carry the name *and* a run of figures — "Overview ·
  38 Cities · 134 Projects" — and those figures were the Overview's own
  headline ("38 cities. What changed?") and its own stat pair, said twice
  more on the same screen.
- **Right: where the reading goes next.** A real link to the next view in
  the reading circuit, carrying that view's number and name. It is the one
  click a reader can make from any view without finding the index, and the
  circuit wraps: on the Essay the strip reads `15 ESSAY › 01 OVERVIEW`.
- **The strip grew from 40px to 44px** because it now carries a control, and
  a control strip holds a 44px target. The cue measures 123.17px wide
  (111.17px of ink plus 6px of side padding on each side) by 44.00px high.
- **The strapline above it now says what the view is about.** The plate
  bar's top-left label was "ASCN · Overview", "ASCN · Demonstrations",
  "ASCN · Public Evidence" — a second name for the view the strip below it
  already named, and in English in every edition. It now carries the same
  one-line description the band uses, translated, so the bar says what this
  is and the strip says where you are, and neither repeats the other.

### The route line is one line, and the hierarchy runs the right way

The first version of the strip put the two facts at opposite ends of the
band. An independent review measured what that cost: at 1440 the position
and the destination were **1005.58px apart**, and the gap was produced by
exactly one token — `margin-left: auto` on the cue. The review's own
falsification removed that token and left **20.00px** — the strip's flex
gap — which proved the metre of nothing was the auto margin and nothing
else. On the build that ships, the same injection leaves **0.00px**:
`.locator` no longer carries a `gap` at all, because the flex gap was
removed with it, and the hinge between the two pairs is now the cue's own
`margin-left: 1.4rem` = 18.19px and nothing else. The void was not a margin
the reader could use; it was the container's spare room holding two halves
of one sentence apart.

The cue now sits next to the position it leaves from, and the strip reads as
a route rather than as two notices. The number→name gap inside each pair is
**9.09px (0.7rem)** — the path band's own gap for the same pair, so the two
routes on the site are measured alike. The hinge between the two pairs is
**18.19px (1.4rem)**: enough to read as a step, not enough to read as a
second band.

The hierarchy was also inverted, and the review caught it by squinting: the
view you were **in** was drawn in `--muted` and the view you were **not** in
was drawn in `--ink`, so the only legible word in the strip was the
destination, and on a phone — where the index rail is not rendered at all —
the reader's own position was the dimmest thing in the chrome. It now runs
the other way, and each colour has one job:

| Part | Colour | Contrast on `--paper` | Job |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The view you are in | `--ink` #f5ecc2 | 14.95:1 | the brightest thing in the strip |
| Its number | `--muted` #a49e83 | 6.60:1 | an address, not a claim — as in the rail |
| The next destination | `--accent` #78cdd0 | 9.68:1 | a link, and the way forward |

`--accent` is declared in the stylesheet for *wayfinding and links only*,
and after this round it is used in exactly two components — the chrome's
cue and the path band's destination names, three rules in all (the cue's
colour, the destination name, and the rule that divides the steps on
hover). The two components are the same role — "this is where the reading
goes" — so the colour now means one thing across the site. Amber left the
band entirely (see below).

Three smaller findings, all closed by measurement rather than by taste:

- **The focus ring crossed the arrow.** The ring is drawn inset (`outline:
  2px solid; outline-offset: -2px`), so its stroke occupies the box's first
  **0–2px** — the outline's rectangle coincides with the border box, and an
  earlier draft of this record put the stroke at "box + 2 → box + 4", which
  is wrong; the seventh review caught it by decoding the pixels of the
  focused cue. Jost's `→` had a *negative* left side bearing, so the
  glyph's ink began 2px before its own box and the ring landed on top of
  the arrow. The arrow is now a drawn path (a 9 × 9 SVG chevron, exact
  geometry, no side bearing) and the cue carries 6px of side padding, so
  the ring's inner edge (box + 2) stops **5.4–6.3px** short of the
  chevron's first ink pixel (box + 7.70) and clear of the last letter
  (box − 6) as well. It is the padding that saves it, not the inset.
  Measured: `ringClearsArrow: true` at every width.
- **A phone had no programmatic "you are here".** The rail is not rendered
  below 860px, so the strip is the only thing on a phone that says where
  the reader is, and it said it in pixels alone: the cue's accessible name
  was "02 Innovation", with no "next" in it, and the strip had no role and
  no name. The position now carries `aria-current="page"`, the strip is a
  `role="group"` named "Reading position", and the cue's name says what it
  is — "Next: Innovation" (TH: "ถัดไป: โครงการ", ZH: "接下来: 项目").
- **The strapline was hidden over a band of viewports for no reason.** It
  was hidden below 600px, and the reason written beside it — that the
  locator below already named the section — died with the old text, because
  the two now say different things. The first repair moved the hide to
  440px, derived from a single string — the Overview's "Scope and method",
  122.39px — and the fifth review found the result: between 441 and 565 the
  bar was serving a label it could not fit, so "Regional dispatches" reached
  the reader as "Regional dispatche…". A label is said whole or not at all;
  a shredded one is worse than silence.

  So the threshold is the **worst label, not one label**, measured across all
  sixteen views in all three editions, and the meeting record's label was cut
  from a sentence to a label first ("Luang Prabang · 2026"; the view's own h1
  already says "9th annual meeting"). The longest remaining is the Insights
  view's "Cross-cutting analysis" at 152.3px. The bar's other items are not
  the same at every width — the official-site link appears above 600px and
  the way back disappears above 860px — so the room the strapline has is
  piecewise constant, and the hide has to clear the tightest band:

  | Band of window | The longest label needs | The band starts at | Slack |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | 481–600px | 469px | 481px | 12px |
  | 601–860px | 588px | 601px | 13px |
  | 861px and up | 511px | 861px | 350px |

  The hide moved to **480px**. Swept across 16 views × 3 editions at every
  width from 440 to 760px, no label is ellipsised or overflows in any
  edition — and at 390px the strapline is gone entirely, which is the point:
  it is said where it can be said whole and is not said at all where it
  cannot.

### What the strip's empty right side is, and is not

The strip is 1237px wide at 1440 and its route is 236px of it; the space to
the right of the route is **1000.66px**. That number is in this record on
purpose, because the previous version of the strip had a 1005.58px gap in
the *middle* of the route and that one was a defect.

The difference is what the space separates. The old gap sat between the two
halves of one relation — "you are here" and "go here next" — so the reader
had to cross a metre of nothing to pair them, and the review's own
falsification showed the whole thing was one CSS token. The new space sits
*after* the end of a line, and the strip's 44px height is the touch-target
floor for the cue it holds, not a height the content asked for. Nothing was
added to fill it, because filling it would be the invented space this round
is about.

An earlier draft of this record justified that by comparison: it called the
strip's space "the same trailing space the plate bar's own label has". That
was wrong, and the sixth review caught it. Measured on the real bar at
1440, the plate bar's trailing space is **0.00px** and its slack is a
**772.45px middle gap** between the strapline and the language switch,
because `.locale` carries `margin-left: auto`. The two are not the same
device — one is after the end of a line, the other is inside the bar — so
the comparison is dropped rather than reworded. The strip's space stands on
its own argument: it is the space a 44px touch target occupies, and the
bar's is the space a right-aligned control cluster occupies.

## The six questions, and what a filter does to them

The News view's open questions were a grid of six peers, and the fourth
review found two HIGH defects in it and one in the measure it used. All
three were the same mistake: the layout was reading the wrong number.

**The column count fell as the window grew.** The view is not as wide as the
window: at 861px the index rail appears (203px) and the view falls from
808px to 632px in a single pixel. The old thresholds (726px, 1096px of
view) sat on the far side of that step, so the count over window 760 → 955
ran **1 → 2 → 1 → 2**: a reader who widened the window by one pixel watched
six cards collapse from three rows to six.

**Law IV broke under the view's own filters.** The grid is `.slice(0, 6)`
of a *filtered* list. With "all topics" selected, six divides by one, two
or three. Selecting one city left one, two or three questions in a
three-column grid — one card in a 1211px row, and two empty cells beside
it. Law IV is a statement about the count, not about the width, and the
review's recommendation was the house's own rule: *set the column count
from the item count*.

**The measure was stated in the wrong unit.** 75ch of Jost is 585px, which
holds about 102 **characters** — 36% past the ceiling rule 3 was written
for. The `ch` unit is the advance of the digit zero (7.80px at 13px) while
the average glyph in these paragraphs is about 5.64px, so `ch` overstates
the character count by about **1.38×**. The measure is now 54ch — 421.2px,
which measures **74 characters** on the longest rendered line.

So the renderer publishes the count and the stylesheet reads it. The
**count** chooses the column count and the **width** only says how many
columns are available: one, two or three. Two questions get two columns,
four get two, six get three, three get three where three are available and
one where they are not, and one or five get one — 5 is prime, and five
columns would need 2110px of view. The width says how many columns *fit*;
it never chooses the count.

This is not the rule the previous draft of this record described. That draft
said "the largest divisor of the count that fits the width", and the sixth
review was right that the two are not the same thing: a count of four could
take **four** columns of 288px — 51.1 characters, inside the house's band —
and fill the row completely. The implementation takes **two** columns of
421.19px instead, and does so on purpose: four columns of 45 characters
would need 1437.7px of view, so the fourth column would arrive and leave
with the window, which is the same class of defect — a column count that
steps with the window — that the sixth review raised as the original HIGH
finding. The implementation admits at most three columns so that the count
cannot step at all. The cost of that choice is recorded below.

one card is taller than its five peers — the question that covers eleven
cities has a three-line heading — and its rule sits 32.5px below the
rules of its two neighbours. That is the honest shape of the content, and
the alternative (aligned rules) is the 50.13px of invented space.

## The instrument was widened, and a second one was built

Review three's most useful finding was F3: the instrument reported "0
findings" while real defects were live, because its scope was the view plus
its direct children and 360px was never among its widths.

- **`scripts/audit-sh.mjs`** now sweeps 360 × 800 as well, and tests every
  descendant for two new classes. A **frame** is a visible box that closes
  around content — a fill, or a border on a closing side — whose width
  exceeds 3.27× its ink. A **prose rule** is a lone bottom rule under prose
  whose rule runs more than 1.26× its ink, which is the house's own written
  rejection of the "failed table" pattern.
- The exclusions are principled, not per-class: canvases, maps and charts;
  **data cells** (inside a `<table>`, or a cell in a grid of three or more
  tracks — the house's own rule that a column width in a data table is a
  data decision, not a stretched box); and **data marks** (a box whose width
  IS the data). A lone rule ABOVE a heading is a section divider.
- Widening the existing stretched-item test to every descendant was tried
  and reverted: it produced 85 false findings by flagging ordinary card
  cells. The two scopes are kept separate — the calibrated test stays on the
  view's top level, the new detectors reach everything.
- The instrument now reports **0 findings** on all fifteen views at all six
  widths (360 / 390 / 1024 / 1440 / 1920 / 2103).

- **`scripts/audit-media-order.mjs`** is new, and it answers the question the
  360px overflow raised: *why* did a narrow layout not apply? It finds
  responsive rules that a later rule in the cascade overwrites. Its first
  version reported four dead rules — and all four were wrong, because it
  tested one viewport. A `max-width: 900px` rule that hands over to a
  `max-width: 600px` rule at 390px is not dead; it governs 601–900px, which
  is the band it was written for. The instrument now enumerates the widths
  where the breakpoint boundaries fall and calls a rule dead only when it is
  overridden at **every** one of them.
- **A third check was added after the third review: the closed band that
  opens inside itself.** A band sized to its own content (`width:
  fit-content`) has said "this is as wide as what I hold"; a child that then
  stretches past its own words has reopened the band inside it. The path
  band's label was a 334.97px box for 32.94px of ink — **10.17×** — and none
  of the four existing checks could reach it: the stretched-item check only
  fires on grid and flex items, the stranded check only on sections, the
  frame check needs a border on two sides, and the prose-rule check skips
  anything with a top border. A green light that does not cover the element
  the round introduced is not a green light. The instrument now reads the
  stylesheet's own `width: fit-content` rules out of the CSSOM, finds those
  bands in the page, and judges every text block inside them against the
  same 3.27× ceiling.

  The check has teeth, and was falsified rather than assumed: a copy of the
  build was served with the label's `width: fit-content` removed, and the
  instrument reported **90 findings** — one per view per width — naming the
  element (`P.path-kicker 127px inside a closed band for 33px of ink

  the same defect the plate bar's kicker had and the same rule that fixed
