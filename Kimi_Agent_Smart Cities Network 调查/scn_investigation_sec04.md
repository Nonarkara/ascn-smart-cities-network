# 4. What SCN Actually Delivers

Smart Cities Network's website declares "13 Key Projects" and "7+ Years of Impact."[^1017^] The chairman's biography describes advisory roles across multiple countries, a UN-Habitat partnership, and capacity-building engagements throughout ASEAN. For an organization incorporated in August 2018, these claims suggest a prolific track record.

The evidence tells a different story. Of the 13 "Key Projects" on SCN's homepage, only one has a verifiable delivered outcome — and that one was implemented by a third-party vendor, not by SCN. The UN-Habitat partnership, while genuine on paper, has produced no identifiable deliverables after two and a half years. The organization's academy is a non-functional website shell. After seven years, SCN has not produced a single original publication or independently organized a major conference.

This chapter examines what SCN actually delivers — and what it does not.

## 4.1 The "13 Key Projects" Claim

SCN lists 13 "Partner Cities" on its website — Singapore, Bangkok, Jakarta, Kuala Lumpur, Manila, Iligan, Santa Rosa, Cauayan City, San Jose Partido, Phitsanulok, Kulai, Iskandar Region, and Cagayan Valley.[^1017^] The presentation implies these are 13 delivered projects. They are not. SCN counts MOU signings, membership affiliations, and personal consulting engagements as "projects" — a classification unrecognizable to any legitimate project management framework.

### 4.1.1 Cauayan City: One Partially Substantiated Case — Delivered by Graffiquo

The single most tangible outcome connected to SCN's network is the Cauayan City digital twin in the Philippines. Graffiquo, a Singapore-based geospatial solutions provider, delivered a 3D digital twin subsequently used to assess PHP 600 million (~USD 12.5 million) in typhoon damage, identify 7,724 families requiring assistance, and coordinate distribution of 36,824 relief packs.[^1187^] The project earned an AWS "Global GameChanger" award.[^1185^]

SCN did not deliver it.

According to Graffiquo's own account, the company was "introduced to the city through a mutual Smart Cities Network contact."[^1185^] WeGO sponsored services worth approximately USD 92,000.[^1183^] Tay Kok Chin promoted Cauayan City on various platforms.[^1039^] SCN's role was that of a connector — a matchmaker between a city and a vendor.

Facilitation is a legitimate network function. The problem is the presentation. SCN lists Cauayan City as one of its "13 Key Projects," implying organizational delivery. The digital twin was built by Graffiquo. The funding came from WeGO. The city partnership was facilitated through the Philippines DOST and Isabela State University.[^1168^] SCN made an introduction — not a project delivery.

The other Philippine city partnerships fare worse. Santa Rosa City and Iligan City both signed "capacity building" MOUs with SCN in 2023.[^1069^] Eighteen months later, neither has any documented outcomes attributable to SCN. The DOST's own Smart and Sustainable Cities Framework was developed with DICT, not SCN.[^1030^]

### 4.1.2 Phnom Penh: A Personal Advisory Role, Not Organizational Delivery

Tay Kok Chin lists himself as "Consulting Expert for the Implementation Masterplan 2035 for the Phnom Penh Smart and Sustainable City Strategic Roadmap."[^1001^] The roadmap exists as a genuine Cambodian government document.[^1167^] But the engagement appears to have been a personal advisory role, not an SCN organizational project.

The UN report "Cities for All" documents Phnom Penh's roadmap priorities without mentioning SCN or Tay Kok Chin.[^1167^] The ASCN Monitoring and Evaluation Report 2025 lists Phnom Penh's "Build4People" project with no SCN credit.[^1010^] The ASCN M&E Report 2023 identified Phnom Penh's focus as public transport efficiency with JICA support — again, no SCN mention.[^1060^] Academic papers on Cambodia's smart city development do not cite SCN.[^1139^][^1141^]

If Tay Kok Chin did advise Phnom Penh, it was a minor personal engagement. SCN's website presents it as part of the network's portfolio.

### 4.1.3 Vietnam: Private Consulting for an Undisclosed Property Developer

Tay Kok Chin was "appointed as the Master Consultant for a property developer in Vietnam to develop Smart Communities" in August 2020.[^1001^] The property developer's name has never been disclosed. There is no Vietnamese media coverage, no government document referencing the engagement. The claim appears only in Tay's self-authored biographies.

This was private consulting for a real estate developer — not a city-level or government project. It belongs in a personal CV, not an organization's roster of "Key Projects." Vietnam's actual smart city development is extensively documented through the Ministry of Information and Communications' ICT Reference Framework and ASCN monitoring reports for Da Nang, Hanoi, and Ho Chi Minh City — none mention SCN.[^1029^][^1060^][^1045^]

### 4.1.4 Iskandar Malaysia: The UK Global Future Cities Programme

SCN lists "The Smart Integrated Mobility and Management System (SIMMS) Program with Iskandar Malaysia" as one of its smart mobility projects.[^1114^] The SIMMS program is real. SCN's involvement is not.

SIMMS was developed by the UK Prosperity Fund's Global Future Cities Programme with the Iskandar Regional Development Authority (IRDA).[^1138^] A webinar on SIMMS featured the GFCP Iskandar Malaysia team — no SCN involvement was mentioned.[^1138^] MIDA documents list SIMMS as a key ongoing project with no SCN credit.[^1140^] The implementation strategy was developed by the UK Global Future Cities Programme, not SCN.[^1138^]

SCN appended its name to a project it had no role in developing.

### 4.1.5 Cambodia E-Tuk-Tuk: A JICA-Developed Initiative

SCN lists "Smart Eco-Tourism in Siem Reap, Cambodia, where transitioning to electric vehicles is one of the components" among its projects.[^1114^] The Siem Reap Smart City Roadmap does include EV promotion.[^1142^] But the roadmap was developed by JICA — SCN is not mentioned in the document. The ASCN Monitoring and Evaluation reports list Cambodia's projects with implementing partners including JICA, ADB, and the Australia-ASEAN Smart Cities Trust Fund — no SCN.[^1010^][^1045^][^1060^]

This pattern repeats across all 13 "Partner Cities." Singapore, Bangkok, Jakarta, and Kuala Lumpur appear to be counted based on geographic proximity or generic network membership. Phitsanulok, San Jose Partido, Kulai, and Cagayan Valley are MOU partnerships without documented outcomes. Of the 13 "Key Projects," one — Cauayan City — has a verifiable delivered outcome that SCN facilitated but did not implement. The rest are personal consulting engagements, partnerships with no results, or claims of involvement in projects led by entirely different organizations.

## 4.2 The UN-Habitat CIF Partnership — Paper Only

If the 13 "Key Projects" represent SCN's marketing face, the UN-Habitat Cities Investment Facility (CIF) partnership represents its credibility backbone. The claim is prominently displayed: SCN serves as "Implementation Partner for the Cities Investment Facility" under UN-Habitat.[^1003^] The partnership is real. Its output is not.

### 4.2.1 A Legitimate CIAP Partner — Verified

On June 22, 2022, SCN signed an MOU with UN-Habitat to serve as a Cities Investment Advisory Partner (CIAP) for the CIF.[^1069^] The UN-Habitat CIF Feasibility Fund Prospectus (October 2023) confirms SCN as one of five CIAP partners alongside Resilient Cities Catalyst, Gensler, Reall, and South Pole.[^1025^] SCN is described as "A global platform aiming to build inclusive, sustainable and smart cities."[^1025^]

This is not a fabricated credential. The UN-Habitat listing is genuine, and the CIAP role — partners that "operate on a non-profit basis to significantly de-risk and take projects to a mid-stage level of project preparation readiness" — is a defined position within the CIF framework.[^1025^]

### 4.2.2 Zero Deliverables After Two and a Half Years

What has SCN actually delivered through this partnership? Nothing that can be identified.

The CIF website and project pipeline pages, reviewed across 2022-2025, do not credit SCN with any specific bankable project identification or scoping.[^1022^][^1107^] The CIF General page describes projects in Kenya, Rwanda, Malawi, India, Ethiopia, Nigeria, and other locations — none mention SCN involvement.[^1022^] No CIF reports, case studies, or pipeline documents specifically name SCN as having delivered any project preparation service.

SCN's own website acknowledges the absence obliquely: its SIMMS project role is described as being "to identify the bankability of the project as part of our role to identify bankable projects with the UN-Habitat's Cities Investment Facilities (CIF) initiative" — but provides no evidence that any such identification was ever produced.[^1114^]

The CIF Feasibility Fund notes typical budgets of up to USD 700,000 per supported project for CIAP partners.[^1025^] There is no evidence SCN has received or managed any of these funds. No bankable projects. No investment facilitation. No published outcomes. After two and a half years, the partnership exists exclusively in the MOU that created it and the prospectus that lists it.

### 4.2.3 Partnership as Credentials Stamp

The UN-Habitat CIF partnership illustrates SCN's broader operational model: accumulate partnership agreements and advisory titles that create the *impression* of legitimacy and activity without requiring measurable outcomes. The CIAP role is a low-barrier advisory platform. SCN is not a lead implementing partner — it is one of several advisory organizations listed in a prospectus. There are no performance clauses requiring a minimum number of bankable projects. No reporting requirements with public accountability. No expiration or enforcement mechanism visible in the public documentation.

The partnership provides SCN with a UN affiliation that can be cited in biographies, on websites, and in presentations to government officials. It does not appear to require SCN to deliver anything in return. This is not a criticism of UN-Habitat's program design — CIAP partnerships may well be structured as open advisory networks. The criticism is of SCN's presentation: "Implementation Partner for the Cities Investment Facility" implies active delivery, while the evidence shows passive listing.

## 4.3 Events and Content

The user's original complaint centered on events with "no content." The research strongly supports this characterization — with important nuances. SCN and its chairman participate in legitimate, well-organized events produced by third parties. But SCN's own content output is minimal to nonexistent.

### 4.3.1 Never Independently Organized a Major Conference

After extensive searching, no evidence was found of SCN independently organizing a major conference, summit, or substantial workshop.[^dim06^] SCN's event participation falls into three categories: moderation and speaking roles at other organizations' events (ConnecTechAsia, Smart City Expo Kuala Lumpur, ERIA dialogues, UN events); co-organization of smaller workshops; and virtual webinar participation during the COVID-19 period.

These are not trivial engagements. Smart City Expo Kuala Lumpur 2025 is a major government-backed event organized by MDEC and Digital Nasional Berhad under Malaysia's Ministry of Digital.[^8^] ERIA's "Realizing Smart Cities" dialogue series is a legitimate multilateral initiative.[^1^] UN-APCTT's events are genuine United Nations programs.[^6^]

But SCN is always a guest, never a host. The content is produced by event organizers, not by SCN. The audiences are gathered by MDEC, ERIA, or the UN — not by Smart Cities Network. SCN is not a convener that draws audiences through its own content or reputation. It is a participant that secures speaking slots through personal networking and partnership credentials that are themselves largely paper-based.

The contrast with the official ASEAN Smart Cities Network is instructive. ASCN's inaugural meeting at the World Cities Summit in 2018 drew 200-plus guests.[^11^] Its governance workshops bring together 75 delegates from 25 cities with published information papers and documented feedback.[^11^] ASCN produces the ASEAN Smart Cities Framework, city-specific action plans, a Smart City Financing Toolkit, and annual M&E reports.[^1010^][^1045^][^1060^] SCN produces none of these.

### 4.3.2 SCN Academy: A Non-Functional Shell

SCN operates a website at smartcitiesnetwork.academy that presents itself as an online learning platform.[^1064^] A direct examination of the site reveals something very different.

The platform is a Moodle-based Learning Management System with exactly three "courses" listed: "Members," "SCN Members Zone," and "Digital Twins in Smart Cities."[^575^] All three list "System Administrator" as the teacher — not a named instructor, not a subject matter expert, but the default Moodle administrative account. The "Members" course contains only generic descriptive text about what an SCN Member Portal "typically includes" — placeholder language, not actual educational content. There is no pricing, no syllabus, no enrollment information, and no guest access. The site appears to be a template installation with descriptive filler text where course content should be.[^dim11^]

This is not an under-resourced educational initiative. It is a non-functional shell — a website that describes what a learning platform should offer without actually offering it. SCN claims to provide "capacity building" to its partner cities.[^1069^] The SCN Academy is the infrastructure that would deliver that capacity building. It does not function.

### 4.3.3 No Original Publications After Seven-Plus Years

Since its incorporation in August 2018, SCN has produced no original white papers, research reports, case studies, or technical publications.[^dim06^] Its only content outputs are presentation slides delivered by Tay Kok Chin — descriptive summaries of publicly available information, not original research.

A sample presentation, "Lessons from Smart Cities Implementations in Asia" (40 slides), covers India's Smart Cities Mission, Singapore's approach, and generic "critical success factors."[^1^] The content is surface-level. There is no data analysis, no original research, no technical rigor. A 31-slide presentation at an Indian academic symposium in 2020 followed the same pattern.[^1^]

SCN has no LinkedIn company page, Facebook page, Twitter/X account, Instagram presence, or YouTube channel.[^dim06^] For a modern "network" organization, this absence is unusual. The organization's primary "platform," according to Tay Kok Chin's own 2019 presentation, is a private WhatsApp group — "invitation only."[^1^] A closed messaging group is not a knowledge-sharing platform. It is a networking channel. Networking is not content.

The only published academic work connected to SCN's chairman is a 2018 IEEE conference paper on the "Garuda Smart City Framework" — developed with Indonesian academics at Institut Teknologi Bandung, not by SCN.[^776^] It predates the CIAP partnership by four years.

### 4.3.4 "Human Smart Cities Methodologies": Borrowed from an Academic Paper

In a 2021 presentation to the Smart City Institute Japan, SCN presented a slide titled "HUMAN SMART CITIES METHODOLOGIES" — capitalized and presented as a framework.[^574^] The slide's fine print, however, reveals the source: "Human smart cities methodologies Kumar et al. (2016), in turn, presents the result of the redevelopment process developed in Srirangapatna, India, through design thinking."[^574^]

The methodology is not SCN's. It is a 2016 academic paper by Dr. T.V. Kumar and colleagues about human-centric smart city approaches in a single Indian town. SCN has not developed any frameworks, standards, or methodologies adopted by any city. The actual standards governing smart cities in ASEAN are the ASEAN Smart Cities Framework (ASCF), national frameworks such as Malaysia's Smart City Framework and the Philippines' DOST Framework, and ISO standards — none of which involve SCN.[^1021^][^1150^]

The "ABC Framework" that SCN promotes — Awareness, Building Capabilities, Commitment to Collaborate — is similarly not a substantive methodology.[^1^] It has no detailed implementation guide, no peer-reviewed validation, and no evidence of systematic application in any city project. It describes generic networking objectives at a level of abstraction that renders it operationally meaningless. The naming convention — "ABC" — suggests a marketing-oriented approach rather than rigorous technical development.

---

The cumulative picture is of an organization that has mastered the accoutrements of legitimacy without mastering the substance of delivery. SCN signs MOUs, not contracts. It lists partnerships, not projects. It presents at conferences, but does not organize them. It claims an academy, but does not operate one. It cites a UN partnership, but has not delivered through it. It references "Human Smart Cities Methodologies," but did not create them. After seven years, the organization's primary verifiable output is a single introduction — connecting Cauayan City to Graffiquo — that produced a successful digital twin SCN did not build, fund, or implement.

This is not to say SCN delivers nothing. It delivers presence: its chairman at industry events, its logo on partnership documents, its name in MOUs and advisory listings. For city officials under pressure to demonstrate smart city engagement, this presence has value — a visible affiliation to cite as evidence of progress. But the user's complaint about "no content" is not a misperception. It is an accurate description of an organization whose business model is the accumulation of credentials that require no content to maintain.
