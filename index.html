ASCN Operational Intelligence · Applied Digital Twins

From static reports to living cities: 5 operational digital twins

What is an urban digital twin? Rather than a static PDF report or a conventional map, an urban digital twin connects a city’s 3D spatial geometry with live sensor feeds, weather models, telemetry, and civic infrastructure. It enables city operators, emergency teams, and citizens to track real-time conditions — from canal gates and rainfall radar to air quality and traffic — as they unfold on the ground. Innovation-as-a-Service

Live systems, not a roadmap. Two national watches, three country dashboards, seven ASCN city dashboards — each one built inside a city, on data the city already had.

Active Innovation Sandbox

What this actually is

Not a product. A method.

Innovation-as-a-Service sits inside a city agency and builds the one screen that speeds up the one decision that actually gets made — on data the city already has, with no new hardware, in two weeks.

One screen. One decision. Two weeks.

  1. PurposefulOne problem that matters to real people.
  2. PracticalBuild on what the city already owns.
  3. Prove itPilot and proof of concept before any large contract.
  4. PeopleIncentives right, so the users want it.

Featured · National watches

FloodDash and Airdash

The two systems the rest of the portfolio is built on. Both are live, free, and run on public data that already refreshes on its own.

flood.nonarkara.org

FloodDash — Thailand Flood Watch เฝ้าระวังน้ำท่วมประเทศไทย

● Live

Every Thai province on one flood picture. Nine public sources — DDPM, Hydro-Informatics Institute, the Royal Irrigation Department, the Meteorological Department and the Pollution Control Department — streamed and refreshed every 3 to 60 minutes.

It is not more sensors. It is the data, cooked: ingest, normalise, persist, score, explain. A resident opens My Area and sees the latest evidence, why it matters, and shelters to confirm with the local authority. Roughly 70,000 pieces of customised information cover every village in Thailand.

  • 9 public sources, one picture
  • 3–60 min refresh window
  • 70,000 village-level records
  • 1784 official disaster hotline
Open FloodDash ↗

air.nonarkara.org

AirDash — Your lungs today ปอดของคุณวันนี้

● Live

A 24/7 air-quality and PM2.5 watch that ranks every province in the country on the same public data, so a number in Udon Thani can be compared honestly with a number in Bangkok.

Same discipline as FloodDash: one metric, one method, every province, no exceptions. What you see is what the public sources say, with a three-day forecast so the trend is visible before the number turns.

  • 24/7 public-data watch
  • 77 provinces, one ranking
  • 3-day forward forecast
  • PM2.5 as the headline metric

Provenance

Where this came from

None of the rows above began as a dashboard. The method behind them was not designed in a summit working group — it came out of two hackathons and one week standing in front of a wall of screens in Taipei.

  1. 2020 · Expo 2020, Dubai

    Hydrogence

    A flood-warning platform built by Thai practitioners that won on the world stage at the Hackathon at Expo 2020. The first version of the argument this page makes: a city should not need to buy software in order to see a flood coming.

    Techsauce ↗ depa Thailand ↗

  2. 2023 · 24 September, Taipei

    Refill City

    A Team of Excellence in the Presidential Hackathon International Track, awarded by the President of Taiwan. Free clean drinking water run by municipalities under their own rules — Nakhon Si Thammarat, then Rangsit, then Sukhothai Thani — and the depa Smart Living Excellence award in 2026. This is where the 4Ps above come from: a prototype, a pilot, then the boring work of changing the rules.

    The Kommon ↗

  3. 2023 · 25 September, Taipei

    The Taipei IOC

    The same week: the Taipei Intelligent Operation Center, and a city dashboard released open-source under AGPL-3.0. A whole city on one screen, used by the people who decide to spend the money. That is where every dashboard on this page comes from.

    Taipei City Dashboard ↗

The public record · 28 April 2018 → 2026

Since 2018, counted

ASCN was founded in 2018. This review covers 2018–2026. This page counts what was committed to it, what it holds, and what can be shown to have reached a citizen — separating money announced from money moved, and activity held from outcome delivered.

Method

Every figure below carries its source and its evidentiary status. Committed is not disbursed. An output is not an outcome.

The first finding is the accounting

Before you can ask whether ASCN worked, you have to ask what ASCN spent. Across the published record since 2018, that question cannot be answered from the public record — not because the money did not move, but because nobody is obliged to say.

USD 0

Held centrally by ASCN

ASCN is a coordinating platform, not a funding pool. It coordinates, it does not finance. Every figure below is money that flows around the network, through bilateral programmes, trust funds and development banks.

0

Systems tracking total flows

The network has no systematic tracking of total investment flows. There is no published ASCN-wide figure for money committed, disbursed or absorbed in eight years of M&E cycles — because the network does not collect one.

13.4%

Of tracked projects completed

134 projects tracked in the September 2025 M&E cycle. 18 completed. 108 still ongoing. A project counted as "ongoing" in year five has not delivered, and the report does not say how long it has been ongoing.

Membership and project counts are quoted here at their stated cut-offs, which differ by source: 35 cities at 30 September 2025 (M&E 2025), 38 at January 2026 (official ASCN roster, after Dili joined with Timor-Leste's accession).

00

The founding instruments · 2018–2019

The instrument that would have measured this never arrived on time

Before the count of projects, the count that matters is the count of the machinery built to evaluate them. The founding documents tell a precise story about why seven years of this network went unmeasured.

Apr 2018

Concept Note

Establishes ASCN as a Singapore proposal. Scope capped at "up to three cities from each AMS". 26 pilot cities listed as of 23 March 2018. No project target. No funding total. No deadline beyond the 2025 planning horizon.

Jul 2018

26 Smart City Action Plans

Each city submits a SCAP with four parts, including exactly two priority projects. That is the entire founding portfolio: 26 cities × 2 projects = 52 projects. Cities were to report status annually to the Secretariat; no such consolidated report has been found.

Aug 2019

Terms of Reference adopted

One year in, ASCN has still 26 cities. The Terms of Reference mandate periodic project evaluation under an M&E Framework — which is recorded in the same report as not yet finished, and deferred to 2020.

2020 →

The accountability gap

The instrument that would have made the network measurable against its own goals slipped past the year it was needed. Every cycle since has counted projects instead — because that is what the framework was built to do.

Sources: ASCN Concept Note (2018), Consolidated SCAPs (as of 8 July 2018), ASCN 2019 Annual Meeting Report. Note that three files in the source library — 09, 13 and 14 — are the same 2019 report in different revisions, not three independent sources. One further document in the library, a Thai Cabinet submission, is an image-only scan with no extractable text and could not be cited.

Projects accumulate faster than they finish

Strip out the frameworks and the toolkits and ASCN reports one thing about itself in every cycle: how many projects exist, and what share is finished. Five data points, four reports, one direction.

The portfolio more than doubled in four years — 65 to 134 projects, a 106% increase. The completion rate improved from 2.6% to 13.4% — more than fivefold, and the strongest positive trend in the entire record. It is still true that eight in ten projects tracked in 2025 have not finished.

Both facts are real. The second is the one that matters to a resident, and it is the one ASCN has never put on a slide.

The 2022 and 2023 reports state no reporting period at all; the 2024 and 2025 reports state one. Cut-offs drift from mid-August to late September, so the four cycles are not a perfectly uniform annual series. The 2025 figure of 134 is verifiable twice within a single document: stated in prose, and reconcilable from 116 appendix-A items plus 18 appendix-B items.

Private correspondence stays local. Public evidence is available in the Archive.
Public reporting rules and first-hand disclosure

Rule, 2019

The host country pays

The Terms of Reference adopted in 2019 are explicit: "the hosting of the Meetings and the cost of organising shall be borne by the host country." The written rule is not that donor governments fly officials around — it is that each host funds its own year.

USD 0

Cost of the network itself

No budget line, staff number or secondment figure appears in any founding, chairmanship or M&E document. This is a structural blank, not a search failure. ASEAN official time has been spent since 2018, and none of it is priced anywhere.

0

Participants, for any event, in any year

No M&E cycle states an attendance figure for a single meeting. Workshops, high-level meetings, showcases, Smart City professional programmes, MoU signing trips and business-matching programmes are all named as recurring activity. Not one of them is counted.

First-hand disclosure · one named witness

The platform's own author has attended ASCN workshops, high-level meetings, showcases, Smart City professional programmes, MoU signing trips and business-matching programmes on a repeated basis over seven years, and estimates travelling more than 200,000 miles in the process.

This is offered as a single witness statement, not as a network statistic, and no number above is derived from it. It is included because it makes the disclosure problem concrete: the network's most frequent beneficiary is a person who can produce his own travel log, while the network itself cannot produce a single participant number for anything it has organised.

Only independently published material is included in the public archive. Redaction alone does not make correspondence public.

Ledger · Agreements & communications

The most productive output, by volume

The reviewed archive is dominated by statements, reaffirmations and partnership announcements. These are real outputs. They are also the easiest output to produce, and the one least connected to a citizen's morning.

3

Memoranda of understanding, in four cycles

Three MoUs are named across the 2022–2025 reports, all in 2023, and all at city level: Makassar Puskesmas with two Indonesian medical partners, and the Siem Reap Tourism Club with a Japanese firm. No network-level MoU, joint declaration or communiqué is named in any cycle.

0

Peer-review exercises ever run

The 2022 report recommends that peer review and expert guidance could give cities opportunities. The 2024 and 2025 reports record no such exercise being conducted. A network that reviews its cities' plans only in its own documents does not review them.

2 of 27

Independent scrutiny in the reviewed archive

Two of 27 curated records are independent critique: a commentary on Makatizen and smart-city governance, and the CIDOB analysis of Singapore's leadership role — the most important auditing document in the whole record, and not an ASCN publication.

Counts describe this archive, a growing research file and not a census of all ASCN activity. They are the ratio of documented scrutiny to documented output — not a verdict on the whole network.

D

Ledger · Training & courses

People were trained. The record stops there.

Training is the network's most human output and the best documented — which is exactly why its blind spot is worth naming: the archive reports attendance, and almost never reports what a graduate then did.

144 + 40

ASPP: trained and enrolled

The ASEAN Smart City Professional Programme with Seoul National University, funded by the ASEAN-Korea Cooperation Fund, reports 144 local government representatives through short-term training and 40 more mid-degree. It is the network's most credible human output and its most precisely counted.

0

Reported downstream outcomes

Across the 2024 and 2025 cycles the programme reports attendance and enrolment only. No graduate is tracked to a system they commissioned, a policy they changed, or a budget they moved. The conversion from trained to deployed is the one metric the network does not publish.

This is the cheapest thing to fix and the most useful. A network that tracked where 184 trained officials went would have a performance story its own reports currently cannot tell — and it is a stronger answer to critics than any toolkit ever published.

?

The founding question

Who was the network built for?

ASCN was established in Singapore in April 2018, as a priority deliverable of that year's ASEAN Chairmanship. Its founders stated the rationale in ASEAN-wide terms — 90 million more people expected to urbanise by 2030, and middleweight cities of 200,000 to 2 million expected to drive 40% of regional growth. On the documents, this was framed as a shared regional problem, not a Singaporean one. The instrument built to solve it, however, carries a different signature.

What the design does reveal

Scope was capped at "up to three cities from each AMS". Decisions are by consensus. Meetings are funded by the host. There is no budget, no staff line, and no working group named in any founding document. Every one of those choices is cheap for a city-state with a permanent secretariat, a consolidated budget and a domestic mandate — and expensive, or impossible, for a country still building those things.

In year one the network added no cities at all: 26 at founding, still 26 at the end of 2019. A body created to scale cities did not scale in its first year.

What the evidence does not establish

It does not establish that Singapore's own density motivated the initiative — the founding documents name Singapore as a co-beneficiary, not the driver. Independent researchers do read the network critically: CIDOB examines Singapore's city diplomacy and commercial interests in its December 2025 analysis, and Kong & Woods (2021) describe a technocratic regionalism in which Singapore's urban model circulates as neutral regional knowledge.

CIDOB / Melinda Martinus, Dec 2025 ↗

Singapore is the only fully urbanised state in the network: every resident lives in a city, and no other member reaches 80%. The median ASEAN member sits near 40% — below half. A network designed by a state where urban governance is national governance is being asked to serve a region where it is not. That asymmetry is real, and it is worth naming precisely enough that it survives a reader who checks the sources. The looser claim — that Singapore is the only urbanised ASEAN state — is not supportable, because Malaysia and Brunei both clear 70%.

Source: World Bank, World Development Indicators, SP.URB.TOTL.IN.ZS, 2023. Malaysia's 76.4% sits alongside 58% forest cover, which looks contradictory until you read the definition: each country classifies a settlement as urban on its own national rule, and Malaysia's census counts a place of 2,000 or more residents where at least half the workforce is in non-agricultural work — so a small township ringed by forest counts. The numbers above are not measured on a common basis, which is itself a reason to treat any "only one urbanised state" comparison as unsettled.

The open question is narrower and more answerable: since its founding in 2018, did the network move practice between cities, or only document it? That has never been tested with a shared dataset. This platform exists to build one.

Meanwhile, in the gap

While the apparatus above ran its cycles, one practitioner inside the network kept shipping. Fifteen systems are live right now — two national watches, three country dashboards, seven ASCN city dashboards and three public platforms — built outside the network's own machinery, in about three years, on public data only.
